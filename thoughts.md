On the animated-cursor SVG gallery concept: it's not inherently wrong, but it's risky for this audience and worth separating the "delight" layer from the "credibility" layer.

On the artsy cursor-follow gimmick:
- For a designer/creative-director portfolio, this reads as a skill demo. For an MBA grad targeting GM/marketing roles, the evaluator is asking "can this person think clearly and drive outcomes," not "can this person build a slick interactive site." A cute cursor-following avatar risks reading as distracting from substance rather than adding to it — especially for GM roles, which skew more corporate/conservative than marketing roles do.
- It's not zero-value though — for marketing/brand roles specifically, showing some visual/interactive taste is on-brand, since that's literally adjacent to the job (creative execution, attention to detail, brand feel). For GM-track roles it's closer to noise.
- My take: keep it, but make it a subtle signature touch, not the primary interaction model. E.g., a small illustrated avatar that reacts on the landing page is charming; needing it to navigate or making it the "site UI" risks it feeling gimmicky or even slowing down the recruiter, who's skimming fast.

On the card → click → route structure:
This part is genuinely good and not too artsy. Cards grouped as Client Work / Personal Work / About / Case Studies / Brand Teardowns, each drilling into its own page (/work/xyz) is just a clean portfolio IA — recruiters in marketing/consulting see this pattern in "case study" portfolio sites all the time. Keep this regardless of what happens with the cursor SVG.

On case studies — images of slides vs. embedding PDFs:
Go with rendered slide images (one image per key slide, not the whole deck dumped), not an embedded PDF viewer. Reasons:
- PDFs force horizontal/full-deck scrolling and a "download to actually read" feel, which kills the fast-skim experience recruiters want.
- Images let him curate — show only the 4-6 slides that carry the narrative (problem framing → analysis → recommendation → "how far we got" / result), skip filler slides, and add his own annotations/captions around them explaining context ("this is where we identified X," "judges pushed back on Y here").
- It also lets him write the surrounding narrative in his own voice — case studies are as much about "how I think" as "what the slide says," and a slide image can't convey his reasoning; the page copy around it does.
- Caveat: if a deck is confidential (client work, actual case comp materials under NDA), redact figures/client names in the images rather than skip the case study entirely — the thinking matters more than the real numbers.

Suggested case study page template:
1. One-line context: competition/client, role, team size, outcome (e.g., "Reached finals of X case comp, 200+ teams")
2. Problem statement (in his words, 2-3 sentences)
3. 3-5 key slide images with short captions explaining the "why" behind each
4. Outcome/result + one reflection line on what he'd do differently
